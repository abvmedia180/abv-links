The whole thing in one picture

A hospital runs on two separate chains of command that only meet at the patient's bedside. One chain is the doctors. The other is the nurses. Around them sit a few support teams that handle money, logistics, and life-after-discharge. Once you see these three groups, everything else clicks.

★ The one rule that explains everything

Doctors and nurses do not report to each other. A nurse manager cannot overrule a doctor's medical order, and a doctor cannot fire or discipline a nurse. They are two independent ladders that answer to two different bosses (the Chief Medical Officer and the Chief Nursing Officer) and only come together to care for the same patient. When people say a hospital is "siloed," this is what they mean — and it's by design, so that clinical accountability stays clear.

Part 1

🩺The Doctor Side

Doctors own the medical decisions — the diagnosis, the plan, the orders, the discharge. Legally, one physician's license is on the line for each patient. Here's that chain, from the person at the bedside up to the top.

The Attending

"the quarterback"

The fully-licensed physician who owns your case. Every other doctor and provider on your case is either helping the attending or reporting to them. If you remember one role, remember this one — the attending is the single point of accountability for the medical plan.

Can do

  • Make the final call on diagnosis, treatment, and discharge
  • Write and change any order (medications, tests, procedures)
  • Bring in specialists ("consults") for extra opinions
  • Overrule a trainee or an NP/PA working under them

Can't do

  • Direct nursing staff's assignments or discipline them
  • Be everywhere — relies on the team to be their eyes and ears
  • Override another attending's decisions on a different specialty

The Hospitalist

a type of attending

A doctor who only works inside the hospital (they have no outside clinic). For most patients admitted for a medical problem, the hospitalist is the attending. Think of them as the hospital's in-house general physician who quarterbacks the day-to-day and coordinates the specialists.

Can do

  • Serve as the attending of record for medical admissions
  • Coordinate all the specialists involved in one patient
  • Manage the whole hospital stay start to finish

Can't do

  • Perform surgery or specialty procedures themselves
  • Follow you after discharge — they hand off to your outpatient doctor
Why it matters: that generic "Attending" box in the big picture is, most of the time, a hospitalist.

Specialists / Consultants

Doctors trained deeply in one area — heart (cardiology), bones (orthopedics), blood vessels (vascular), ear-nose-throat (ENT), surgery, plastics, hormones (endocrinology), and so on. Usually they advise the attending. But sometimes — especially for surgery — the specialist becomes the attending and runs the whole case.

Can do

  • Give expert recommendations in their area
  • Perform specialized procedures and surgeries
  • Become the lead attending when the reason for admission is their specialty

Can't do

  • Manage the whole patient outside their lane (they defer to the attending)
  • Get involved unless they're formally consulted
Rule of thumb: if you're admitted because of surgery, the surgeon is often the attending. If surgery is just one piece of a bigger medical picture, the hospitalist stays in charge and the surgeon consults.

Doctors in Training

"house staff"

Real doctors (they have their MD or DO) who are still completing their training inside the hospital, always under an attending's supervision. Two tiers: Residents come straight out of medical school; Fellows have finished residency and are training in a subspecialty.

Can do

  • Examine patients, write orders, and prescribe
  • Do a lot of the hands-on, hour-to-hour doctoring
  • Perform procedures they've been signed off on

Can't do

  • Take final responsibility — the attending co-signs and owns the plan
  • Practice independently until training is finished and they're board-certified
Timeline: Med school → Residency (3 years for internal medicine or pediatrics; 5+ for surgical fields) → optional Fellowship (2–3 more years to subspecialize, e.g. a cardiologist is an internal-medicine doctor who did a heart fellowship).

Chief Medical Officer

CMO

The top doctor in the building — the boss of the medical staff, usually with an associate/deputy underneath. They set standards and handle the big escalations, but they stay out of routine day-to-day cases and defer to the attending's judgment.

Can do

  • Set medical policy and quality standards for the whole hospital
  • Step in on serious safety issues or disputes

Can't do

  • Micromanage individual cases (that's the attending's job)
  • Be your first call — that would skip the whole chain below them
≠
Worth un-learning: "Doctor in training" doesn't mean student. Residents and fellows already graduated med school and treat patients every day — they just work under a supervising attending until their training is done. The medical student is a separate, earlier stage that isn't even on this chart.
Bonus context

How a new doctor gets placed: "The Match"

You noticed this and you had it basically right. When medical students finish school, they don't just apply and get hired. They go through a national algorithm called The Match.

Applicants rank the programs they want
A student lists hospitals in order of preference — dream program #1, backup #7, and so on.
Hospitals rank the applicants they want
At the same time, each program ranks its favorite candidates. Neither side sees the other's list.
A computer matches the two lists
A famous, fairness-tested algorithm (it won a Nobel Prize in economics) pairs everyone at once. You might land your #1 — or your #5.
The stakes are brutal
Match once a year on the same day nationwide. Not matching means no training spot at all — after four years of school and six figures of debt. The most competitive fields (like orthopedics) are the hardest to match into.
The in-between role

NPs and PAs — the "advanced providers"

These are highly-trained clinicians who aren't doctors but do a lot of doctor-like work: examining patients, ordering tests, and writing prescriptions. Hospitals group them under an umbrella label. You'll hear APP (Advanced Practice Provider) most places; some systems, like the one on your whiteboard, use their own term, ACP (Advanced Clinical Provider). Same idea, local branding.

Nurse Practitioner

NP

Started as a registered nurse, then earned a graduate degree to practice at an advanced level. An NP is trained around a patient population — e.g. family, adults, kids, or psychiatry.

Can do

  • Diagnose, treat, and prescribe (including controlled meds)
  • Manage patients fairly independently in their population

Can't do

  • Freely jump to a different population (e.g. adult → pediatrics) without more schooling and a new certification

Physician Assistant / Associate

PA

Trained in a condensed, general version of medical school — the "mini med school" idea. Because that training is generalist, a PA can move between specialties over a career (surgery this year, dermatology the next) picking it up on the job.

Can do

  • Diagnose, treat, and prescribe (including controlled meds)
  • Switch specialties without going back to school
  • First-assist in surgery, run clinics, manage inpatients

Can't do

  • Practice with zero physician involvement (rules vary by state)
↺
The correction on your board: you had the gist, with one nuance. An NP isn't locked out of ever switching specialties — it's that they're certified for a population (adults, kids, psych…), so a big switch needs new training. The PA's flexibility is the real headline: general training in, easy specialty-hopping out. And both, not just doctors, can write prescriptions.
Part 2

👩‍⚕️The Nursing Side

If doctors decide the plan, nurses carry it out and watch the patient 24/7. This is its own ladder with its own boss (the Chief Nursing Officer). Read it top-to-bottom — the person at the bottom is at the bedside; the person at the top runs nursing for the whole hospital.

TOP
Chief Nursing Officer (CNO)
Runs all of nursing for the hospital. The nurse equivalent of the CMO.
↓
Director of Nursing
Oversees several units or a whole service line. Reports to the CNO.
↓
Nurse Manager
Runs one unit or floor — staffing, budget, quality, the buck stops here for that unit.
↓
Assistant Nurse Manager (ANM)
The manager's right hand. Often the most reachable person when something needs fixing now.
↓
Charge Nurse
An experienced RN running today's shift on the floor — assigning patients, solving hour-by-hour problems. A role, not a permanent rank.
BASE
Registered Nurse (RN)
At the bedside. Gives meds, monitors, assesses, and is the constant human link between patient and doctor.
≠
The correction on your board: you had LPN and PCA drawn as the bottom rungs below the RN. That's the common misread. They're not lower rungs of the nurse ladder — they're different jobs with different scopes that support the RN. Here's the honest version:

Licensed Practical Nurse

LPN

A licensed nurse with a focused scope. Does more than an aide but works under an RN or provider's direction.

Can do

  • Give many medications
  • Basic wound care, collect data, monitor patients

Can't do

  • Do the full independent assessment or complex care an RN owns
  • Hang certain high-risk IV medications (rules vary)

Patient Care Assistant

PCA · aide

Unlicensed support staff — the hands that keep patients safe and comfortable. Essential, constant, and often the first to notice a problem.

Can do

  • Vital signs, bathing, mobility, feeding
  • 1-to-1 "sitting" with fall-risk or at-risk patients

Can't do

  • Give medications
  • Remove IVs or do any licensed clinical task
Part 3

🧩The Support Teams

Not doctors, not nurses — but nothing moves without them. These teams handle logistics, money, and what happens after the hospital.

Case Management

The discharge logistics and insurance engine. Often nurses by background. They plan the exit from day one: where does the patient go next, who pays, and what equipment do they need.

Handles

  • Insurance approvals and "is this covered?"
  • Booking rehab, nursing-home, or home-health placement
  • Home equipment (oxygen, walker, hospital bed)

Doesn't

  • Make medical decisions
  • Provide counseling or crisis support (that's Social Work)

Social Work

The human/psychosocial side. They step in when a patient's situation is complicated — emotionally, financially, or for safety — and connect people to resources.

Handles

  • Counseling and crisis support
  • Complex or unsafe home situations, abuse/neglect concerns
  • Resources for homelessness, substance use, financial hardship

Doesn't

  • Handle insurance authorizations day-to-day (that's Case Management)

Bed Management

The hospital's air-traffic control for beds. Small team, huge operational power: they decide who goes where and when a bed opens up.

Handles

  • Assigning and tracking every bed in the building
  • Admissions from the ER and transfers between units

Doesn't

  • Make clinical calls — but they control the flow everyone depends on
↺
The correction on your board: you split it as "Social Work = rehab, Case Management = home." In practice it's the other way around and fuzzier: Case Management owns the logistics and insurance for all destinations (rehab and home), while Social Work owns the complicated human situations. Both work on getting the patient safely out the door — just from different angles.
Part 4 · how it connects

If something's wrong, who do you go to?

This is the most useful thing to internalize. You almost never jump straight to the top. You climb the ladder one rung at a time — it's faster, it keeps the right people informed, and skipping steps makes you look like you don't understand the system.

Start at the bedside
The RN, the NP/PA, or the resident. 90% of things get solved right here.
Then the Nurse Manager (or their assistant)
For anything the bedside can't fix. Even when it's outside their lane, keeping them aware is smart.
Then the Attending
For a genuine change in the medical plan. Never your first move — loop in the team first.
Then the department director / hospitalist lead
The right stop before you ever bother the C-suite.
Only then, the CMO
Approachable, but use sparingly. Going here without looping in the attending first reflects badly on you, not them.
★
The relationships worth building: Bed Management, Nurse Managers, and especially the Assistant Nurse Managers. They're often the most reachable and the most able to make something happen the same day.
Put it together

One patient, start to finish

Watch all the roles show up in order for a made-up patient — call her Ms. Rivera, admitted overnight from the ER with pneumonia.

ER → a bed
Bed Management finds her an open bed on a medical floor and assigns it.
A team forms
A hospitalist becomes her attending. A resident and an NP or PA handle the hour-to-hour. An RN is assigned to her at the bedside.
Daily care
The RN gives meds and monitors; a PCA takes vitals and helps her walk; the doctors round each morning and adjust the plan.
A complication
Her heart rhythm looks off. The hospitalist consults cardiology — a specialist advises, but the hospitalist stays in charge.
Planning the exit
From early on, Case Management lines up home oxygen and checks insurance. She lives alone and is anxious, so Social Work arranges support at home.
Discharge
The attending signs off, the RN gives instructions, and she goes home with a follow-up booked. The hospital team hands her back to her outpatient doctor.
