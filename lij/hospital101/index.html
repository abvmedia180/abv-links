Hospital 101

A plain-English guide to how a hospital actually works — who's who, what each person can and can't do, and how it all fits together around one thing: the patient. No clinical background needed.

The whole hospital in 30 seconds

A hospital runs on two separate chains that meet at the patient: doctors decide the medical plan, nurses deliver the care. They don't report to each other.

Around them, support teams handle beds, discharge, pharmacy, lab, and the building. When something's wrong, you climb the ladder one step at a time — bedside first, never straight to the top.

Everything else here — specialties, imaging, reimbursement — is just detail hanging off those few ideas.

★ The one rule that explains everything

Doctors and nurses do not report to each other. A nurse manager cannot overrule a doctor's medical order, and a doctor cannot fire or discipline a nurse. They are two independent ladders that answer to two different bosses (the Chief Medical Officer and the Chief Nursing Officer) and only come together to care for the same patient. When people say a hospital is "siloed," this is what they mean — and it's by design, so that clinical accountability stays clear.

Part 1

🩺The Doctor Side

Doctors own the medical decisions — the diagnosis, the plan, the orders, the discharge. Legally, one physician's license is on the line for each patient. Here's that chain, from the person at the bedside up to the top.

The Attending

"the quarterback"

The fully-licensed physician who owns your case. Every other doctor and provider on your case is either helping the attending or reporting to them. If you remember one role, remember this one — the attending is the single point of accountability for the medical plan.

Can do

  • Make the final call on diagnosis, treatment, and discharge
  • Write and change any order (medications, tests, procedures)
  • Bring in specialists ("consults") for extra opinions
  • Overrule a trainee or an NP/PA working under them

Can't do

  • Direct nursing staff's assignments or discipline them
  • Be everywhere — relies on the team to be their eyes and ears
  • Override another attending's decisions on a different specialty

The Hospitalist

a type of attending

A doctor who only works inside the hospital (they have no outside clinic). For most patients admitted for a medical problem, the hospitalist is the attending. Think of them as the hospital's in-house general physician who quarterbacks the day-to-day and coordinates the specialists.

Can do

  • Serve as the attending of record for medical admissions
  • Coordinate all the specialists involved in one patient
  • Manage the whole hospital stay start to finish

Can't do

  • Perform surgery or specialty procedures themselves
  • Follow you after discharge — they hand off to your outpatient doctor
Why it matters: for a medical admission, that "Attending" box is filled by either a hospitalist (in-house) or a private attending (below) — one or the other, never both.

The Private Attending

a type of attending

A community physician with their own outpatient clinic who admits their own patients and stays their attending in the hospital. Unlike a hospitalist, the hospital isn't their home base — they come in to see their patients (often early in the morning) and move in and out between the hospital and their clinic. They're not offsite all day; they're just not continuously onsite the way a hospitalist is.

Can do

  • Be the attending for their own patients — set the plan and orders
  • Bring in specialists and manage the whole stay
  • Carry the relationship from clinic → hospital → back to clinic

The catch

  • Not continuously on-site — in and out during the day, so harder to reach at any given moment
  • A group partner covers only when they're truly away (vacation, or if a patient switches off them) — not as a routine daily hand-off
The practical difference: a hospitalist is in the building the whole shift; a private attending comes in to round — early, or in and out — but isn't there continuously. A group partner steps in mainly when they're actually off, not during a normal day.

Specialists / Consultants

Doctors trained deeply in one area — heart (cardiology), bones (orthopedics), blood vessels (vascular), ear-nose-throat (ENT), surgery, plastics, hormones (endocrinology), and so on. Usually they advise the attending. But sometimes — especially for surgery — the specialist becomes the attending and runs the whole case.

Can do

  • Give expert recommendations in their area
  • Perform specialized procedures and surgeries
  • Become the lead attending when the reason for admission is their specialty

Can't do

  • Manage the whole patient outside their lane (they defer to the attending)
  • Get involved unless they're formally consulted
Rule of thumb: if you're admitted because of surgery, the surgeon is often the attending. If surgery is just one piece of a bigger medical picture, the hospitalist stays in charge and the surgeon consults.

Doctors in Training

"house staff"

Real doctors (they have their MD or DO) who are still completing their training inside the hospital, always under an attending's supervision. Two tiers: Residents come straight out of medical school; Fellows have finished residency and are training in a subspecialty.

Can do

  • Examine patients, write orders, and prescribe
  • Do a lot of the hands-on, hour-to-hour doctoring
  • Perform procedures they've been signed off on

Can't do

  • Take final responsibility — the attending co-signs and owns the plan
  • Practice independently until training is finished and they're board-certified
Timeline: Med school → Residency (3 years for internal medicine or pediatrics; 5+ for surgical fields) → optional Fellowship (2–3 more years to subspecialize, e.g. a cardiologist is an internal-medicine doctor who did a heart fellowship).

CMO

Chief Medical Officer

The top doctor in the building — the boss of the medical staff, usually with an associate/deputy underneath. They set standards and handle the big escalations, but they stay out of routine day-to-day cases and defer to the attending's judgment.

Can do

  • Set medical policy and quality standards for the whole hospital
  • Step in on serious safety issues or disputes

Can't do

  • Micromanage individual cases (that's the attending's job)
  • Be your first call — that would skip the whole chain below them
≠
Worth un-learning: "Doctor in training" doesn't mean student. Residents and fellows already graduated med school and treat patients every day — they just work under a supervising attending until their training is done. The medical student is a separate, earlier stage that isn't even on this chart.
The in-between role

NPs and PAs — the "advanced providers"

These are highly-trained clinicians who aren't doctors but do a lot of doctor-like work: examining patients, ordering tests, and writing prescriptions. Whether someone is an NP or a PA, they're grouped under one umbrella term you'll hear constantly: ACP — Advanced Clinical Provider. So "call the ACP" just means "get the NP or PA." Either way, they always work as part of the team under a supervising attending — they extend the attending's reach, they don't replace them.

Nurse Practitioner

NP

Started as a registered nurse, then earned a graduate degree to practice at an advanced level. An NP is trained around a patient population — e.g. family, adults, kids, or psychiatry.

Can do

  • Diagnose, treat, and prescribe (including controlled meds)
  • Manage patients fairly independently in their population

Can't do

  • Freely jump to a different population (e.g. adult → pediatrics) without more schooling and a new certification

Physician Assistant / Associate

PA

Trained in a condensed, general version of medical school — the "mini med school" idea. Because that training is generalist, a PA can move between specialties over a career (surgery this year, dermatology the next) picking it up on the job.

Can do

  • Diagnose, treat, and prescribe (including controlled meds)
  • Switch specialties without going back to school
  • First-assist in surgery, run clinics, manage inpatients

Can't do

  • Practice with zero physician involvement (rules vary by state)
Part 2

👩‍⚕️The Nursing Side

If doctors decide the plan, nurses carry it out and watch the patient 24/7. This is its own ladder with its own boss (the Chief Nursing Officer). Read it top-to-bottom — the person at the bottom is at the bedside; the person at the top runs nursing for the whole hospital.

How it works here: the RN is backed up by either an LPN or a PCA — one or the other, never both on the same unit (which one depends on the floor and how much licensed support its patients need). They both work under the RN, but they're two different jobs with different scopes — one licensed, one not. Here's what each can actually do:

Licensed Practical Nurse

LPN

A licensed nurse (about a one-year program) who works under the direction of an RN or a doctor. Real license, real hands-on scope — but the higher-judgment calls aren't theirs. On our floors they typically wear dark-green scrubs.

Can do

  • Give most medications, including into an existing IV line
  • Start and remove a regular ("peripheral") IV, and draw blood from it
  • Wound care, catheters, suctioning, dressing changes
  • Collect data, take vitals, monitor, and chart

Can't do

  • The full nursing assessment, triage, or care plan — that's the RN's
  • Push a medication straight into a vein ("IV push")
  • Give blood or run a transfusion
  • Anything requiring independent clinical judgment
The nuance: the IV limit isn't "no IVs" — an LPN can start and remove a peripheral IV and give most IV fluids and meds. What they can't do is the fast "IV push," blood products, or the assessment and care plan.

Patient Care Assistant

PCA · aide

Bedside support staff (often a certified nursing assistant) who work entirely under the RN — the hands and eyes at the bedside, and often the first to notice something's wrong. On our floors they typically wear maroon scrubs.

Can do

  • Vital signs, blood-sugar finger sticks, weights, intake & output
  • Bathing, feeding, toileting, turning, helping patients walk
  • 1-to-1 "sitting" with fall-risk or at-risk patients
  • Transport, stocking, keeping the room safe and clean

Can't do

  • Give any medication — none, ever
  • Remove IVs or manage any IV line
  • Assessments or clinical judgment — they report what they see to the RN
One variation: some hospitals train this role (calling it a "PCT," Patient Care Technician) to also draw blood and run EKGs. That extra scope depends on the facility — but the "no medications" line never moves.
Part 3

🧩The Support Teams

Not doctors, not nurses — but nothing moves without them. The ones you'll deal with most around a patient's path are the discharge pair — Case Management (getting the patient home) and Social Work (getting them to a facility) — plus Bed Management, which runs the flow of patients in and around the building. These are independent services, not a chain — Bed Management isn't a step before the other two. The wider support cast (pharmacy, lab, EVS, patient access, and more) is in Support & service roles →

Case Management

The go-home engine. Often nurses by background. They set up everything a patient needs to be safe and cared for at home after discharge.

Handles

  • Home health aides & personal-care aides (bathing, dressing, daily tasks)
  • Visiting/skilled nurses and home physical or occupational therapy
  • Durable equipment — oxygen, hospital bed, walker, wheelchair, commode
  • Home supplies & services — wound/ostomy supplies, home IV infusion, delivery & setup

Doesn't

  • Arrange rehab or nursing-home placement (Social Work does that here)
  • Make medical decisions or provide counseling

Social Work

Here, Social Work owns discharge planning to a facility. When a patient can't go straight home and needs a rehab center or nursing home, Social Work finds the placement and sets up the transfer. They also handle the harder human situations — safety, counseling, resources.

Handles

  • Placement at a rehab, skilled nursing, or nursing-home facility
  • Coordinating the transfer and its paperwork
  • Counseling, safety concerns, and community resources

Doesn't

  • Set up home aides or home equipment (that's Case Management)
  • Handle day-to-day insurance authorizations
The clean split: "where does the patient go after the hospital?" — home → Case Management, a facility → Social Work.

Bed Management

The hospital's air-traffic control for beds. Small team, huge operational power: they decide who goes where and when a bed opens up.

Handles

  • Assigning and tracking every bed in the building
  • Admissions from the ER and transfers between units

Doesn't

  • Make clinical calls — but they control the flow everyone depends on
✓
This one is hospital-specific. Who owns rehab-vs-home placement genuinely varies building to building. Here, the split is by destination: a patient going home is Case Management (aides, equipment, home services); a patient going to a facility — rehab or nursing home — is Social Work. Some hospitals divide it differently, so confirm it wherever you work.
Part 4 · how it connects

If something's wrong, who do you go to?

This is the most useful thing to internalize. You almost never jump straight to the top. You climb the ladder one rung at a time — it's faster, it keeps the right people informed, and skipping steps makes you look like you don't understand the system.

★
Rule zero — start with your own leadership. Before you escalate above the bedside — to an attending, a director, or anyone higher — check with your own direct supervisor first for guidance on how to handle it. It's expected, they'll often resolve it or point you to the right door, and it keeps you from going around your own chain.
Start at the bedside
The RN, the NP/PA, or the resident. About 65% of things get solved right here — and they can give you context on what's going on, before or after you talk to the patient or their family.
Then the Nurse Manager (or their assistant)
For anything the bedside can't fix. Even when it's outside their lane, keep them aware — they're effectively the patient's primary point person when something goes wrong, almost like a project manager for that patient.
Then the Attending
For a genuine change in the medical plan. Never your first move — loop in the team first.
Then the department director / hospitalist lead
First, speak to your own director to confirm it's necessary and get their guidance on how to navigate it. This is the right stop before you ever involve the C-suite.
Only then, the CMO
Approachable, but use sparingly. Skipping the chain to go straight here can strain the working relationships you rely on every day — be mindful of the bridges you're crossing, and loop in the attending and your director first so no one feels gone around.
Put it together

One patient, start to finish

One made-up patient — Ms. Rivera, admitted overnight from the ER with pneumonia — and every role she meets, in order.

ER → a bed
Bed Management finds her an open bed on a medical floor and assigns it.
A team forms
A hospitalist becomes her attending. The hour-to-hour is handled by either a resident or an NP/PA — one or the other, not both. An RN is assigned to her at the bedside.
Daily care
The RN gives meds and monitors; a PCA takes vitals and helps her walk; the doctors round each morning and adjust the plan.
A complication
Her heart rhythm looks off. The hospitalist consults cardiology — a specialist advises, but the hospitalist stays in charge.
Planning the exit
From early on, Case Management checks insurance and sets up home oxygen and a visiting nurse so she can go home. (If she couldn't manage at home and needed a rehab facility, Social Work would arrange that placement instead.)
Discharge
The attending signs off, the RN gives instructions, and she goes home with a follow-up booked. The hospital team hands her back to her outpatient doctor.

The directory

The specialists — who does what

When the attending needs an expert, they consult a specialty. Here's the full cast you'll hear paged, grouped by how they work, plus the words and procedures that actually get said out loud on the floor.

Group 1The front door & the OR

Emergency Medicine ED / ER

The front door — stabilize anyone, for anything, then decide: home, admit, or transfer.
  • Triage = sorting patients sickest-first
  • Workup = the tests ordered to figure out what's wrong
  • Boarding = an admitted patient stuck in the ED waiting for a bed

Anesthesiology OR · PACU · pain

Keep you asleep, still, and pain-free during surgery; run pre-op clearance and the recovery room.
  • PACU = the recovery room where patients wake up
  • General vs. regional = fully asleep vs. numbing one area (epidural, nerve block)
  • Extubate = remove the breathing tube once they can breathe on their own
Group 2Surgical specialtiesthey operate
Called to the case, and often take a patient to the operating room.

General Surgery

The "belly & soft-tissue" surgeons — abdomen, bowel, gallbladder, appendix, hernias.
  • Lap chole = keyhole gallbladder removal
  • Appy = appendectomy (removing the appendix)
  • Ex-lap = opening the abdomen to find/fix a problem
  • Ostomy = rerouting the bowel to a bag on the belly

Orthopedics Ortho

Bones, joints, muscles, ligaments, tendons. Fractures, hip/knee replacements, trauma.
  • ORIF = setting a broken bone with plates/screws
  • THA / TKA = total hip / knee replacement
  • Closed reduction = setting a bone without cutting
  • Non-weight-bearing = no weight on that limb

Vascular Surgery

Blood vessels everywhere except heart and brain. Poor circulation, aneurysms, clots, dialysis access.
  • AAA ("triple-A") = a bulge in the body's main artery
  • Fem-pop bypass = rerouting blood around a blocked leg artery
  • Fistula / graft = a made access point in the arm for dialysis
  • BKA / AKA = below-/above-knee amputation

ENT Otolaryngology

Ear, nose, throat — plus head, neck, and the airway.
  • Trach = a surgical airway opening in the neck
  • Epistaxis = a nosebleed (and packing it)
  • Laryngoscopy = looking at the voice box with a scope
  • Neck dissection = removing lymph nodes/tumor from the neck

Plastic Surgery

In hospitals, mostly reconstructive — complex wounds, flaps, hand injuries, breast reconstruction.
  • Flap = moving living tissue (with its blood supply) to cover a defect
  • Skin graft = a thin sheet of skin moved to cover a wound
  • Debridement = cutting away dead/infected tissue
  • Wound vac = a suction dressing that speeds healing

Neurosurgery

Surgery on the brain, spine, and nerves. Brain bleeds, tumors, trauma, high pressure.
  • Craniotomy = surgically opening the skull
  • EVD = a drain into the brain's fluid spaces to relieve pressure
  • Shunt (VP shunt) = a permanent tube draining brain fluid to the belly
  • Burr hole = a small drilled hole in the skull

Urology

Urinary tract (kidney-to-bladder plumbing) and male reproductive system.
  • Foley = a tube in the bladder to drain urine
  • Cystoscopy = a scope into the bladder
  • TURP = trimming an enlarged prostate to unblock urine flow
  • Ureteral stent = a tube holding the ureter open (often after a stone)

OB / GYN

Pregnancy and childbirth (OB) plus female reproductive health (GYN). Based in Labor & Delivery.
  • C-section = surgical delivery through the abdomen
  • L&D = the Labor and Delivery unit
  • Preeclampsia = dangerous high blood pressure in pregnancy
Group 3Medical specialtiesthey consult & manage
Brains of a problem — they advise and manage, and mostly don't operate.

Cardiology

The heart — pump, arteries, and electrical rhythm. Interventional opens arteries; EP handles rhythm.
  • Cath / PCI = catheter to the heart's arteries; a stent props one open
  • STEMI = a major heart attack needing emergency artery opening
  • Echo = ultrasound of the heart
  • Pacemaker / ICD = implanted device to pace or shock the heart

Pulmonology / Critical Care

Lungs and breathing; many also run the ICU as "intensivists."
  • Intubation = placing a breathing tube into the windpipe
  • Vent = the machine that breathes for a patient
  • Bronchoscopy = a scope into the airways
  • ICU = Intensive Care Unit; the sickest patients

Nephrology

Kidneys — filtering blood, fluid balance, electrolytes. Runs dialysis.
  • HD = hemodialysis; a machine filters the blood
  • Fistula / graft = the arm access used for dialysis
  • CRRT = slow, continuous dialysis run 24/7 in the ICU
  • AKI = acute kidney injury (sudden drop in function)

Neurology

Brain, spinal cord, nerves — the medical (non-surgical) side. Strokes, seizures, weakness.
  • tPA = a clot-busting drug for certain strokes (tight time window)
  • EEG = a recording of the brain's electrical activity
  • LP ("spinal tap") = a needle in the low back to sample spinal fluid
  • TIA = a "mini-stroke" that resolves

Gastroenterology GI

The digestive tract — esophagus to intestines, plus liver and pancreas.
  • EGD = a scope down to the stomach ("upper endoscopy")
  • Colonoscopy = a scope up through the colon
  • ERCP = a scope that clears blocked bile/pancreatic ducts
  • PEG tube = a stomach feeding tube placed with a scope

Endocrinology Endo

Hormone glands — thyroid, adrenal, pituitary — and, mostly, diabetes. Consult only.
  • DKA = a dangerous high-blood-sugar emergency
  • Sliding scale / insulin drip = dosing insulin to the current sugar
  • Fingerstick / accucheck = a bedside blood-sugar check

Hematology / Oncology Heme/Onc

Blood disorders (heme) and cancer (onc) — usually one combined team.
  • Chemo = drug treatment that kills cancer cells
  • Port = an implanted access point for repeated chemo/draws
  • Neutropenia = dangerously low infection-fighting cells
  • Neutropenic fever = a fever in that state — an emergency

Infectious Disease ID

Serious or complicated infections and the antibiotics to treat them. Consult only.
  • Abx = antibiotics
  • Cultures = growing a sample to identify the bug
  • Sensitivities = which antibiotics will kill that organism
  • PICC for long abx = a durable line for weeks of IV antibiotics

Psychiatry C-L Psych

Mental health on medical/surgical patients — the Consultation-Liaison team. Suicide risk, agitation, delirium, withdrawal.
  • 1:1 (sitter) = staff staying with a patient at all times for safety
  • Capacity eval = can this patient make their own decisions?
  • CIWA = a score tracking alcohol-withdrawal severity
  • Delirium = sudden acute confusion from a medical cause

Palliative Care

Comfort, symptom relief, and matching care to the patient's goals. Runs alongside treatment; hospice is end-of-life.
  • Goals of care = defining what the patient wants treatment to achieve
  • Comfort care = focus shifts entirely to comfort, not cure
  • DNR / DNI = no CPR / no breathing tube
Group 4Procedures, imaging & the lab
Behind the scenes, but their results and tubes drive everything.

Interventional Radiology IR

"Surgery through a pinhole" — needles, tubes, and catheters guided by live imaging, no open surgery.
  • G-tube / J-tube / GJ-tube = feeding tube into the stomach / small intestine / a combined tube (J-route when the stomach isn't safe to feed)
  • PICC line = a long IV from the arm to a big vein near the heart
  • Drain (pigtail) = a tube draining an abscess or fluid collection
  • Embolization = deliberately blocking a vessel to stop bleeding
  • Nephrostomy = a tube through the back draining a blocked kidney
  • Para / thora-centesis = draining fluid from the belly / around the lung

Diagnostic Radiology

The doctors who read the images (IR does procedures). Every scan gets read and reported by them.
  • CT = fast, detailed cross-section X-ray imaging
  • MRI = magnet imaging; best for brain, spine, soft tissue
  • US = ultrasound; sound waves, no radiation
  • Wet read = a quick preliminary read for urgent cases; formal report follows

Pathology / Lab

Examine tissue, blood, and fluids under the microscope to make the diagnosis. Rarely seen, but results drive care.
  • Biopsy = a tissue sample taken to examine for disease
  • Frozen section = a rapid biopsy read during surgery
  • Path report = the final microscope diagnosis (benign vs. cancer)

PM&R / PT & OT

Restoring function. PT = movement/walking; OT = daily-living tasks. They also help decide rehab placement.
  • Acute rehab (IRF) = intensive inpatient rehab (~3 hrs/day)
  • SNF ("sniff") = Skilled Nursing Facility; lower-intensity recovery
  • ADLs = basic self-care tasks (dressing, bathing)
  • Dispo = the plan for where the patient goes next
!
Three that trip people up: "D/C" means discharge OR discontinue — context decides. Palliative ≠ hospice (palliative runs alongside treatment; hospice is end-of-life). "Cath" is generic for catheter — a heart cath, a Foley, and a PICC are totally different things.
The rest of the floor

Support & service roles

Not doctors, not nurses, not the placement teams — but the floor stops working without them. These support and ancillary services are the people a new hire sees all day and needs to be able to name.

Pharmacy pharmacists

Far more than a drug counter. They verify every order, but also prepare and mix medications (IV drips, chemo, IV nutrition), dose the tricky drugs (blood thinners, key antibiotics), guide antibiotic use, reconcile a patient's home meds, respond to codes, and counsel patients at discharge.
  • Verify = a pharmacist clears an order before it can be given
  • Med rec = reconciling home meds vs. hospital orders to catch conflicts
  • Stewardship = pharmacists steering the right antibiotic use
  • Compounding = mixing custom IVs, chemo, and nutrition in a clean room
  • Formulary = the hospital's approved drug list

Respiratory Therapy RT

The breathing team — they run ventilators, give breathing treatments and oxygen, assist intubations, and draw arterial gases. Everywhere the sickest patients are.
  • Neb / breathing treatment = inhaled medication mist
  • Vent settings = how the ventilator is tuned for a patient
  • Wean = gradually reducing ventilator support toward breathing alone

IV Team nurses

A specialized group of nurses who place the hard IVs — often using an ultrasound to find deep veins when the bedside nurse can't get access.
  • Difficult access = a patient whose veins are hard to get a line into
  • Ultrasound-guided IV = placing a line using ultrasound to see the vein

Nutrition dietitians

Dietitians set diets and tube-feeding plans; food services delivers the trays. Diets are prescribed like medicine.
  • Diet order = the prescribed diet (cardiac, renal, diabetic, NPO)
  • Tube feeds (enteral) = liquid nutrition through a feeding tube

Patient Navigation transport

Moves patients around the building — to imaging, to the OR, to a new unit — safely and on time. (This hospital's name for the transport team.)
  • Transport = a staffed move of a patient off the unit
  • Portable = bringing the test (e.g. X-ray) to the bedside instead

Environmental Services EVS

Cleaning and bed turnover. Their speed directly controls how fast a bed reopens — so they're tightly linked to Bed Management.
  • Terminal clean = a deep clean of a room after a patient leaves
  • Bed turnover = cleaning and readying a bed for the next patient

Interpreter Services

Live or phone/video interpreters for patients who don't speak English. Using them is often a legal requirement, not a courtesy.
  • Language line = on-demand phone/video interpretation
  • Limited English proficiency (LEP) = the patient needs an interpreter

Chaplaincy spiritual care

Emotional and spiritual support for patients and families of any faith — especially around serious illness and end of life.
  • Spiritual care = non-clinical emotional/faith support

Unit Receptionist

Sits at the front desk of a specific unit — the non-clinical face of that floor. Answers the unit phone, greets and directs visitors, manages paperwork and charts, and coordinates with the nurses at that station.
  • Can do: phones, visitor questions, paperwork, ordering supplies, directing traffic
  • Can't do: anything clinical — no care, no meds, no medical info
  • The desk = the unit's central station where they sit

Lab clinical laboratory

Runs the blood and fluid tests (chemistry, blood counts, cultures) behind the scenes. Nearly every diagnosis and dosing decision waits on lab results.
  • Stat lab = a test needed urgently
  • Resulted = the test is back and posted in the chart

Keeps the physical building and its equipment running — power, water, heating/cooling, and the medical devices themselves (biomed).
  • Work order = a logged request to fix or check something
  • Biomed = the team that maintains medical equipment

The front end of the revenue cycle. They register patients — capturing identity, demographics, and insurance, verifying coverage, and handling consents — so the visit is set up correctly from the first minute.
  • Registration = entering the patient's info and creating the visit record
  • Insurance verification = confirming coverage before or at the visit
  • Demographics = the patient's identity and contact details on file
  • Pre-authorization = getting the insurer's OK before certain services

★ your world

Patient Experience (PX)

The team that measures and improves how care feels to the patient — not the medicine, the experience of it. It's scored, public, and tied to how much the hospital gets paid, which is why it gets executive attention. On the ground, a PX advocate hears patient complaints, runs service recovery, drives process improvement, and tracks the survey data (often through Press Ganey).

  • HCAHPS = the national CMS survey patients get after discharge — the official scorecard for patient experience.
  • Press Ganey = the vendor that runs the surveys and provides the scorecards and dashboards the PX team lives in.
  • Top Box = the share of patients giving the best possible answer (e.g. "Always"), which is what actually counts in the score.
  • Star Rating = the public 1–5 star summary of a hospital's patient-experience scores.
  • Rounding = proactively checking on patients (leaders or nurses) before problems turn into complaints.
  • Service recovery = making it right, fast, when a patient's experience goes wrong.
  • VBP (Value-Based Purchasing) = the CMS program that ties a slice of payment to these scores.
Deep dive · your world

How reimbursement works

Two ideas explain most of it. (Based on current Medicare rules; the specifics shift year to year.)

1 · Paid by diagnosis, not by day

Medicare pays a hospital a fixed amount per admission based on the diagnosis (called the DRG), no matter how long the patient actually stays.

Why length of stay is a money problem
Say a DRG is priced around a 15-day stay. Whether the patient goes home on day 12 or stays 60 days, the base payment is essentially the same flat amount. Leave efficiently → the hospital keeps the margin. A long, complicated stay → the hospital eats the extra weeks. That's why throughput and safe, timely discharge (Case Management, Social Work, Bed Management) is a financial issue, not just a clinical one.
2 · Why patient experience is real money

On top of that base payment, Medicare nudges a hospital's dollars up or down based on quality and experience, and publishes a public 1–5 star rating that shapes its reputation. Patient experience — the HCAHPS survey patients get after discharge — is a big slice of that star rating:

Mortality22%
Safety of Care22%
Readmission22%
Patient Experience22%
Timely & Effective Care12%

The scoring rule worth burning in: on the survey, only the single best answer counts. "Always" is "Top Box" — and "Usually" scores the same as "Never": zero.

3 · The rumor, settled
"If a patient doesn't answer 'Always' on the survey, we lose money for that patient's stay."
Not for that one stay — but the surveys add up to something that matters a lot.
  • That patient is already paid for. The hospital gets a set amount for their diagnosis, and the survey doesn't change it. No single survey cancels a specific bill.
  • Only the top answer counts. The choices are Never → Sometimes → Usually → Always, and the hospital only gets credit for "Always." Even "Usually" counts as a zero. That's the whole reason "Always" matters so much.
  • All the answers add up to a score. The hospital's overall "Always" rate becomes a number, compared against every other hospital — and that number sets a slice of its Medicare payment and its public 1–5 star rating.
  • So it does matter — just not one patient at a time. Across a big multi-hospital system, small differences in that score move real money and reputation.
Deep dive · your world

Pain management

Pain is one place where good clinical care and good patient experience can pull in different directions — which is exactly why it's worth understanding for your role.

What it is, and the goal

Pain management is how a hospital controls pain that routine medicine can't handle alone — after surgery, with cancer, or during an acute flare. Big hospitals have an anesthesiology-led Acute Pain Service for complex cases; Palliative Care handles comfort in serious illness; but most pain is managed by the bedside nurse and the primary team. The modern standard is multimodal — combining opioids with non-opioids (Tylenol, anti-inflammatories, nerve blocks, nerve-pain meds, ice/heat, PT) to control pain with fewer opioids. And the goal is deliberately realistic: tolerable pain and the ability to move, breathe, and sleep — not a pain score of zero. Setting that expectation early is itself a patient-experience skill.

The PCA pump

A PCA pump — Patient-Controlled Analgesia — is a programmed IV pump with a button. When pain rises, the patient presses it and gets a small, pre-set dose. It's safe by design: a fixed dose per press, a lockout (extra presses in that window do nothing), and hard maximum limits. Because only the patient should press it — and an over-sedated patient stops pressing — it has a built-in brake. (Family or staff pressing it for the patient defeats that safety and is a firm no.)

≠
Don't confuse the two "PCAs": a PCA pump is Patient-Controlled Analgesia, a pain device. A PCA on the staffing sheet is a Patient Care Assistant. Same three letters, completely unrelated — and you'll hear both in the same hallway.
Sickle cell & the pain crisis

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is inherited: red blood cells form a rigid, crescent shape, get stuck in small vessels, and block blood flow. When flow is blocked, the result is a vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC) — sudden, severe pain. Because people with SCD are hospitalized often over a lifetime, many are opioid-tolerant and need higher doses to get relief — a reflection of their disease and history. National guidelines call for prompt treatment (first opioid dose within about an hour), frequent reassessment, dosing to the patient's own written pain plan, and a PCA pump for steady control.

i
Why the context helps: a crisis is hard to see from the outside, and these are frequent, high-need patients — so the practical point for your role is simply to follow the individual pain plan and route clinical questions to the care team, rather than read anything into how much medication a patient needs.
The IV Benadryl question

This is the delicate one, so start with the truth: IV Benadryl (diphenhydramine) is a legitimate, common hospital medicine — for allergic reactions, as a pre-med before blood transfusions, and to relieve the itching that opioids often cause (a very real issue in sickle cell care). Most patients asking for it need it for exactly those reasons. The nuance clinicians watch: pushed fast through an IV it can cause a sedating, mildly pleasant feeling, and given IV alongside high-dose opioids it raises the risk of over-sedation — which is why guidelines often prefer the oral form. So when a request is very specific about the drug, the route (IV push), and the speed, the clinical team may take a second look — as a safety check, not a verdict on the patient. The line for your role: "give the patient exactly the drug and route they name" is not automatically the safe answer, and that call belongs to the clinical team — not to front-line experience staff.

★ where experience and safety collide

Hospitals are scored on experience through HCAHPS, and that score touches Medicare payment. HCAHPS used to ask how well staff controlled pain — but in 2018 CMS removed those pain questions from the payment score (clinicians reported feeling pressure to over-prescribe opioids to protect their ratings) and replaced them with "Communication About Pain": how well staff talked with you about your pain and how to treat it. The measure shifted from "did you erase my pain" to "did you communicate about it." That's the whole tension in one change: giving a patient exactly what they ask for can raise satisfaction but be clinically wrong or unsafe; declining appropriately can spark a complaint. Usually safety and satisfaction align — but when they don't, safety wins.

How good teams handle it
  • Set expectations early — the goal is tolerable pain and function, not zero.
  • Explain the plan and the "why" — patients accept "no" far better when they understand the reasoning (which is exactly what the 2018 shift now rewards).
  • Use multimodal approaches so the answer is "here's what we're adding," not just "no more opioids."
  • Be consistent shift to shift — mixed messages drive complaints and erode trust.
  • Honor individualized pain plans, especially in sickle cell — consistency with the plan is both better care and better experience.
  • Route safety concerns to the clinical team — experience staff own the conversation and the emotion, not the drug-and-route decision.
◎
The through-line: you can manage the experience — dignity, communication, responsiveness, believing the patient — without touching the clinical call. Those are two different levers, and good patient-experience work pulls the first hard while respecting the second.

Imaging — MRI, CT, and the cardiac tests

"Imaging" is how the team sees inside the body. The two big scanners — MRI and CT — get confused constantly, and the safety rules around MRI are genuinely life-or-death, so they're worth knowing. (General orientation, not medical advice — clinicians make the calls, and some thresholds are set by each hospital's policy.)

MRI — the magnet

MRI uses a strong magnet — no radiation — and is best for soft tissue like the brain, spine, and joints. The downside: it's slow, loud, and enclosed, so patients who can't hold still (young kids, severe claustrophobia) may need sedation.

⚠ MRI safety — the part that can kill someone

The magnet is always on, and metal becomes a dangerous projectile — so everyone is screened for metal and implants before going in. Pacemakers are the big one: many are fine, but only after the device team sets them to a special mode first. When in doubt, it's a hard stop until radiology clears it.

CT — the fast X-ray

CT (the old "CAT scan") is a fast X-ray that builds cross-section images. It uses radiation, but it takes seconds and is open (not a tube) — so it's the ER's go-to for bleeding, trauma, bones, and emergencies.

MRI vs. CT — when each is used

CT — fast & urgent

  • Uses radiation (X-rays)
  • Fast — seconds; open, not a tube
  • Best for bleeding, trauma, bone, lungs, emergencies
  • The ER's go-to

MRI — detailed & soft-tissue

  • No radiation (magnet)
  • Slow — 20–60+ min; loud, enclosed
  • Best for brain, spine, joints, fine detail
  • Usually scheduled
Cardiac imaging — the heart tests

Cardiology has its own alphabet of tests. Here's what each actually is and when it's used.

Echo / TTE

Ultrasound of the heart from a probe on the chest — shows the heart pumping, valves, and squeeze in real time. The everyday workhorse.
  • No radiation · no sedation · no NPO

TEE

Transesophageal echo — the ultrasound probe goes down the swallowing tube for a much closer look. Used to hunt for clots, inspect valves, and diagnose heart-valve infection.
  • Needs sedation + NPO · no radiation

Stress test

Checks blood flow to the heart under stress — a treadmill on EKG, or medication if the patient can't walk. The nuclear version adds a radiotracer to map blood flow at rest vs. stress.
  • Nuclear = radiation · caffeine held · short NPO

Cardiac cath / angiogram

A catheter threaded to the heart injects dye to see the coronary arteries directly on live X-ray. Also interventional — blockages can be stented in the same trip.
  • Radiation · iodine contrast · sedation · NPO

Cardiac CT / CTA · calcium score

Non-invasive CT look at the heart arteries. CTA uses iodine contrast to image them without a catheter; the calcium score is a quick, no-contrast risk number.
  • Radiation · CTA uses contrast · no sedation

Cardiac MRI

Detailed MRI of the heart muscle — scar, inflammation, precise function. For tricky cases echo can't resolve.
  • No radiation · often gadolinium · full MRI metal screening applies

Holter / event monitor

A wearable rhythm recorder worn for days to weeks to catch an irregular heartbeat a snapshot would miss.
  • Records rhythm, not an image · nothing needed

EKG / ECG

The basic electrical tracing of the heartbeat from stickers on the skin — about a minute. The universal first cardiac test.
  • No radiation · no prep
